History of Artificial Intelligence


From Hand-Written Rules to Machine Learning


Early artificial intelligence focused on symbolic reasoning, assuming human thought could be captured through explicit if-then statements. Expert systems relied on human specialists to write out every fact and rule by hand. While this worked for narrow puzzles, it quickly collapsed. Human knowledge has too many exceptions to write down manually, and the sheer number of interacting rules overwhelmed computers, leading to a major industry downturn known as the AI winter.


To break through this wall, researchers shifted the paradigm from symbolic logic to statistical machine learning. Instead of engineers coding every rule by hand, algorithms were designed to analyze large datasets and discover patterns on their own. This shift from hand-crafted rules to data-driven learning laid the foundation for modern neural networks.


Case Study: The Stanford MYCIN System


MYCIN was an early medical expert system built at Stanford to diagnose bacterial blood infections and prescribe antibiotics. Because medical tests carry natural uncertainty, the designers created certainty factors to score how strongly test results supported a diagnosis. In blind trials, MYCIN actually suggested better treatments than medical faculty members.


Despite its performance, hospitals never adopted it. Doctors did not want to spend a large portion of an hour manually typing lab numbers into an offline terminal. More importantly, the system had zero common sense outside its narrow rule list. If a user entered test numbers for an animal or an inanimate object, MYCIN would treat it like a human patient and prescribe lethal doses of antibiotics.


Connection to Modern AI Systems


Modern AI systems, such as large language models and diagnostic image classifiers, solved the exact knowledge bottleneck that stopped MYCIN. Instead of relying on humans to hand-code medical logic, modern models train on millions of text records and clinical images to learn medical patterns automatically.


However, modern systems still inherit MYCIN's fundamental blind spot. Because they learn through statistical patterns rather than genuine world understanding, modern models still lack true common sense. Just as MYCIN gave confident antibiotic advice for an inanimate object, modern systems can hallucinate false facts or misread images when faced with inputs outside their training data.
