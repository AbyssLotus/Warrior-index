Algorithmic Ethics, Bias, and System Safety


The Math Behind Competing Definitions of Fairness


Turning fairness into code is difficult because different mathematical definitions of fairness clash with each other. Unless two groups have the exact same baseline rate for an outcome, a model cannot treat them equally across every metric. It cannot keep accuracy, false alarms, and missed catches balanced at the same time. Fixing the math for one of these rules will always throw another one off.


Case Study: COMPAS Recidivism Scores and Courtroom Due Process


An investigative audit by ProPublica analyzed these scores and demonstrated that the tool treated defendants differently based on race. The developers defended their software by showing that it followed calibration, meaning that any given score represented the exact same likelihood of reoffending regardless of a defendant's race. However, because historical arrest rates were unequal due to policing patterns, the mathematics guaranteed that keeping the scores calibrated forced the tool to generate unequal error rates between groups.


The legal challenge reached the Wisconsin Supreme Court in State v. Loomis. The court ruled that judges could continue using COMPAS scores during sentencing hearings even though the private company refused to reveal its proprietary formulas under trade-secret laws.


Right now, nobody takes full blame when these tools make mistakes. The software company avoids responsibility by calling the score an advisory suggestion, while judges avoid blame by claiming the score was just one factor among many. If this setup changed even slightly—like requiring judges to follow the score automatically—the court would take full blame for removing human judgment. On the other hand, if courts switched to open-source models, independent data scientists could inspect the code and be held accountable. Moving forward, courts need to take clear steps: stop using secret algorithms in sentencing, require third-party bias testing before buying any software, and give defense lawyers a clear way to inspect the data and challenge bad scores.



Key Finding


  • Proxy Bias: Questions regarding family history and neighborhood crime act as indirect demographic proxies, biasing scores against minority defendants even without an explicit race variable.

Citation
